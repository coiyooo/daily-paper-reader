# ABSTRACT 

External skills provide domain procedures without parameter updates, but exist-ing methods often edit skills directly from failed rollouts without structured rout-ing from an observed failure to an editable location; existing skill graphs also underuse semantic boundaries, object addresses, and topological dependencies for skill retrieval, targeted updating, and scoped validation. We introduce S KIL -

> L

AA ( Skill Abductive Attribution ), a structured skill-optimization framework for frozen language models. It represents skill applicability, execution, and compo-sition in a unified graph, allowing the same structure to support skill selection, attribution-guided repair, and update validation. SKILL AA contrasts successful and failed executions to route candidate repairs to specific graph objects, updates only the selected local structure, and uses Local and Big Gates to screen candidate changes before commitment. With gpt-5.6-sol, S KILL AA reaches 81.5%, 66.7%, and 91.2% on SearchQA, LiveMath, and DocVQA, respectively, and attains the highest observed mean in every main setting. These results support the utility of attribution-guided graph editing and graph-scoped validation. 

Figure 1: From failed rollouts to targeted skill repair. Existing skill systems can organize and compose reusable procedures, but a failed rollout alone does not reveal whether the candidate repair should address a trigger, execution step, exclusion boundary, skill relation, or one-off execution lapse. S KILL AA contrasts successful and failed evidence to select a candidate graph object, edits only that location, and retests affected skills and cases. Rejected candidates are rolled back to the original plan, while validated repairs are retained for subsequent tasks. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Frozen language models increasingly rely on external tools, documents, workflows, and skills to solve tasks (Schick et al., 2023; Patil et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2023). These skills are readable, 1arXiv:2609.20455v1 [cs.AI] 17 Sep 2026 Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 portable, and parameter-free, but reliable improvement requires knowing when they apply, how they interact, where failures arise, and whether an edit causes regressions. Skill research has moved from independent retrieval toward relation modeling. SkillRouter selects skills using query–skill similarity and skill-body signals (Zheng et al., 2026). Graph-of-Skills re-trieves prerequisite-aware bundles (Liu et al., 2026), while SkillDAG, HyperSkill, CaSKG, and SkillGraph learn relations from experience or encode higher-order, causal, and policy-coupled com-position (Bai et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026c;b). These methods show the value of structured relations, but use graphs mainly for selection and composition: node semantics do not specify edit targets, and topology does not determine retest scopes or rollback units. Skill-evolution methods distill experience, workflows, and skills from execution (Zhao et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Ni et al., 2026); ProTeGi, TextGrad, and GEPA optimize linguistic artifacts from scores and trajectory feedback (Pryzant et al., 2023; Yuksekgonul et al., 2024; Agrawal et al., 2025); and SkillOpt optimizes a persistent skill document through bounded edits and Gates (Yang et al., 2026). However, they still lack structured object-level edit routing from failed executions. Flat text mixes activation conditions, procedures, exceptions, and relations, so the same failure may reflect missing knowledge, failed activation, a faulty procedure or relation, or a one-off execution lapse. We therefore ask how structured execution evidence can route candidate repairs to localized graph objects and validate them before commitment .To address this problem, SKILL AA uses one addressable skill graph throughout retrieval, attribution-guided updating, and rollback; Figure 1 illustrates this design through a targeted re-pair. Nodes separate applicability, execution, and exclusion boundaries, while typed edges explic-itly model skill relations, allowing the student to select and compose skills over the complete graph. The teacher then uses successful and failed executions to select a candidate field or relation and proposes only a local edit at that address. Dependent edits are validated as atomic groups by the Local Gate, and the Big Gate decides epoch-level commitment after merging, making graph objects the edit addresses, retest scopes, and rollback units. Across SearchQA, LiveMath, and DocVQA, Full S KILL AA attains the highest observed mean in every main setting; with gpt-5.6-sol it reaches 81.5%, 66.7%, and 91.2%. The ablations support the utility of structured representation, attribution-conditioned editing, and Local-Gate regression control, while the held-out effect of the Big Gate is model-dependent. More broadly, the pipeline is designed not merely to accumulate skill text but to improve the quality of representation, editing, and validation in a persistent external skill state. Our contributions are: 1. Graph-structured representation and relation modeling: separating applicability, exe-cution, and exclusion boundaries, and using typed, explained edges for skill selection and coordinated composition. 2. Attribution-guided edit routing: using successful and failed execution evidence to route candidate repairs to node fields, skill relations, or NO_PATCH .3. Scoped validation and reversible graph updates: using affected-case retesting for local patches and an epoch-level commit rule for merged candidates. 4. Evaluation across task types: multi-model experiments and ablations on question-answering, mathematics, and document-image benchmarks that validate the overall results and the roles of the three components. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Graph-structured skills. Graphs first organized document evidence, memory, and tool relations: ControlLLM models tool dependencies, CRAFT creates and retrieves specialized toolsets, Tool-Rerank uses hierarchical relations to rerank candidates, and GraphRAG and HippoRAG connect distributed knowledge through relational graphs and graph propagation (Liu et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2024; Edge et al., 2024; Gutierrez et al., 2024). These methods establish the importance of skill relations for compositional execution, but mainly target graph construction and retrieval rather than carrying structured objects through targeted attribution, updating, and rollback. 

Skill evolution and controlled optimization. Optimization of linguistic artifacts has progressed from prompt search to trajectory-driven reflection. ProTeGi, OPRO, TextGrad, and GEPA evolve prompts or agent configurations through natural-language critiques, model-based optimizers, propa-gated textual feedback, or complete trajectories (Pryzant et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023; Yuksekgonul et al., 2024; Agrawal et al., 2025). Skill evolution similarly moves from experience accumulation toward persistent optimization: Voyager and ExpeL accumulate skills and cross-task experience, while Agent Workflow Memory, SkillWeaver, ReasoningBank, and Trace2Skill convert execution trajectories into workflows, skills, or reasoning memories (Wang et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2025; Ouyang et al., 2025; Ni et al., 2026). SkillsBench and related surveys characterize skills as reusable procedural knowledge with applicability conditions (Li et al., 2026a; Xu & Yan, 2026), and SkillOpt treats a persistent skill document as an optimizable exter-nal parameter of a frozen agent, trained through bounded edits and multilevel controls (Yang et al., 2026). These approaches improve the controllability of skill evolution, but flat text and candidate-level acceptance still lack field-level edit routing and cannot independently update or roll back skill relations. 3Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027

To obtain comparable local evidence, S KILL AA partitions only the update pool before optimization into fixed, disjoint quadruples using task-type constraints and question-text similarity: 

Group m = {update m, 1, update m, 2, update m, 3, update m, 4}.

Here, m indexes update groups, each containing four update examples. These groups organize contrasts among training examples: successful members provide correct paths for attribution, and the update members of relevant groups enter the Local Gate. The held-out test set is disjoint and is not used for group construction, attribution, patch synthesis, or either Gate. For each failure, the teacher combines verifier feedback, trace , validated usage , and successful group members. It first finds the earliest observable decision after which the answer is unrecover-able, then follows this diagnostic chain: behavior covered? → relevant object activated? → stored semantics correct? 

→ steps or relations complete? → rule followed? The resulting diagnosis follows one of the six routes in Table 1: the first four authorize only the corresponding field- or edge-level G RAPH PATCH , while an execution lapse or insufficient evidence returns NO_PATCH . A patch records its evidence, target, and before–after content; successful cases guard against regressions, and dependent operations form an atomic P ATCH GROUP Jk that is ac-cepted or rolled back as a unit. Here, abductive attribution means selecting the smallest graph-level cause whose corresponding object-level repair can be tested through paired executions. Questions. We evaluate S KILL AA as a graph-space optimizer for frozen language models. The experiments ask whether optimized graphs improve over controlled skill-representation stages, how their results compare with source-protocol references, which components contribute to downstream utility, whether the approach extends to sequential interaction, and how the graph evolves across epochs. 

Models. We evaluate OpenAI’s gpt-5.6-sol, gpt-5.5, and gpt-5.4-mini (OpenAI, 2026); Google’s gemini-3.5-flash (Google AI for Developers, 2026); and Alibaba’s qwen3.8-flash (Alibaba Cloud Model Studio, 2026) and qwen3.6-35b-a3b (Qwen Team, 2026).

Table 2 evaluates gpt-5.6-sol, gemini-3.5-flash, and qwen3.8-flash on the three main benchmarks; each model serves as both student and teacher while optimizing its own graph. Each benchmark uses a disjoint update/test split. The update pool is partitioned into fixed groups of four update examples using only update-question text and task-type metadata; held-out test questions are not used for grouping, graph initialization, updating, or selection. The conditions progress from No skill, Flat skill, deterministically converted Structured skill G0, and G0 + Trace/Usage to full S KILL AA with attribution, targeted updates, and both Gates. Exact split counts and grouping methods are in Appendix E. SkillAA achieves the highest observed mean for every model–benchmark combination, and the pro-gression clarifies where the gains arise. Flat skill generally improves over No skill, showing that the initial skills provide useful task procedures. From Flat skill to Structured skill G0, the structured rep-resentation separates applicability conditions, execution steps, and skill relations, making retrieval and activation conditions clearer while explicitly specifying how skills compose. G0 + Trace/Usage further requires the student to report its execution process and the graph objects used, thereby con-straining the model to organize its solution around the skills; variation across models also shows that observable records alone are insufficient for reliable improvement. Finally, SkillAA improves over G0 + Trace/Usage for every model and benchmark, demonstrating the end-to-end utility of adding attribution-guided updates and validation as a package. Table 4 examines four factors on the Table-A benchmarks. Flat skill is the common baseline, and all parenthesized values show differences from Flat skill. The graph-structure rows compare the con-tribution of structured nodes and explicit relations. The retrieval rows are read-only interventions on the matching self-teacher terminal graph, testing activation boundaries, edge rationales, S–Q re-trieval, and Nearest-Q reuse. The update rows remove structured attribution, correct-case protection, the Local Gate, or the Big Gate under the self-teacher setting to test repair and rollback mechanisms. The final two rows are a separate Teacher-transfer test : they hold the student, data, and three-epoch schedule fixed while replacing only the teacher used for attribution and patch generation. Teacher effects should therefore be read by comparing the two teacher rows for the same fixed student, not from the parenthesized values. Appendix F gives the exact interventions and comparison rules. 

Graph structure ablation. Under the self-teacher reference, removing all edges (Nodes only) lowers performance by 1.8 pp on average, with a larger loss for the gpt-5.6-sol student and mixed effects for gpt-5.4-mini. The optimized nodes nevertheless remain 7.6 pp above Flat skill on average without edges, showing that structured node fields carry much of the benefit. Edges therefore help coordinate already-relevant procedures, but the stronger and more consistent contribution comes from separating positive activation, execution, and exclusion boundaries in the nodes. 

Retrieval mechanism ablation. These results strengthen the activation-boundary explanation. Relative to each student’s self-teacher Full graph, removing when_to_use and avoid causes the largest retrieval-side loss ( 9.4 pp on average), and fixed S–Q retrieval also trails Full ( 7.8 pp ). Nearest-Q reuse has a smaller average loss ( 1.9 pp ), while removing edge rationales is mixed and nearly neutral on average ( 0.6 pp loss). Thus, the most robust retrieval signal is not the edge prose alone, but the node-level contract that tells the student when a skill applies and when it should be rejected. 

Update mechanism ablation. Under the same-student self-teacher reference, replacing structured attribution with a generic failure summary causes the largest update-side drop ( 8.3 pp on average), showing that attribution-conditioned object routing is the main source of repair utility among the tested update mechanisms; this result measures downstream editing utility rather than standalone attribution accuracy. Removing the Local Gate causes the next-largest average loss ( 4.9 pp ), sup-porting affected-scope regression testing, while removing correct-case protection costs 1.3 pp on average. The Big Gate has a mixed, model-dependent effect: it improves the gpt-5.6-sol results by 

1.5/4.6/1.2 pp on SearchQA/LiveMath/DocVQA, whereas removing it improves the correspond-ing gpt-5.4-mini results by 1.1/2.0/0.9 pp . The Big Gate should therefore be interpreted as an epoch-level commit rule that enforces positive update-pool gain, not as a component that uniformly improves held-out accuracy.

When not to update the graph. ALFWorld provides a counterexample to the assumption that further skill optimization always helps. All methods solve every task within 100 steps, while the initial skill performs best under the tighter 50-step budget (Appendix G.1). In this setting, additional graph edits do not improve interaction efficiency. When attribution finds no specific defect in the graph, S KILL AA should therefore return NO_PATCH rather than force an update. 

When to improve the model instead. The epoch trajectories show that later graph patches are sometimes rejected because they provide no net improvement (Appendix G.2). This gives a practi-cal stopping signal for graph editing, not proof of a model-capacity ceiling. If the remaining fail-ures are attributed to reasoning, perception, or execution rather than graph content, further progress requires improving the model. Future systems could alternate the two: update the graph for graph-addressable failures and the model for capability-limited failures. 9Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 

# 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduced S KILL AA, a graph-space skill optimizer that uses one addressable graph to connect skill selection, execution, attribution-guided updating, and rollback for frozen language models. Stable IDs connect node fields and typed edges to semantic activation, edit addresses, affected-case retesting, and atomic restoration. The teacher proposes a localized candidate repair; the Local Gate tests its affected scope, and the Big Gate applies an epoch-level update-pool commit rule. Across three benchmarks and multiple model families, S KILL AA attains the highest observed mean in every main setting. Ablations support structured node semantics, explicit relations, attribution-conditioned editing, and Local-Gate regression control, while the held-out effect of the Big Gate is model-dependent. Current results remain limited by graph scale, task scope, and few repeated runs. Future work should internalize repeatedly validated prompts and skills into model parameters, then alternate model updates with skill-library evolution so that internal capability and external knowledge improve iteratively. 10 Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027

Each epoch runs the current graph on the full update pool: 800 examples for SearchQA and DocVQA, and 468 for LiveMath. Requests may run in parallel, but the graph does not change until all results are collected. The fixed groups are used only to supply related successful cases and to help define the Local-Gate retest scope. The complete G0 rollout is the initial reference. When a candidate passes the Big Gate, its full-pool rollout becomes the next reference; when it fails, both the graph and reference remain unchanged. Cached results are reused only when the graph, rendered prompt, case IDs, and completion status match. 

usage validation. usage is defined as the exact deduplicated set of graph objects that change an intermediate state or otherwise affect the submitted answer, and every such object must also be explained in trace . Validation checks ID existence, edge endpoints, set equality between the two records, and whether each cited object is attached to an explicit input–operation–output transition. A semantic step without an ID, an ID without a state transition, or any transition that violates the execution contract invalidates the record and sends the example to the unknown-usage scope. # H DISCUSSION , L IMITATIONS , AND NEXT STEPS 

H.1 WHAT THE RESULTS ESTABLISH 

The evidence supports a narrower claim than “graphs make agents better.” The graph is useful because it turns skill text into an editable address space. Node fields carry most of the applicability and procedure information; edges help when several already-relevant procedures must be ordered or combined. The ablations are consistent with this division: optimized nodes remain useful without edges, while removing activation boundaries or replacing semantic activation with a fixed retrieval rule loses more. Attribution-guided routing and the Gates form a testable repair pipeline. Routing selects a can-didate graph object, the Local Gate tests that object-level intervention on affected cases, and the Big Gate tests interactions after surviving repairs are merged. Because the six routes occur only on failures and the benchmark supplies no unique gold repair object, the reported evidence con-cerns the operational utility of these paired interventions rather than route-classification accuracy. The residual-error audit illustrates the distinction between graph-addressable defects and theorem, perception, or scorer failures. The negative ALFWorld result gives the practical boundary. When the initial skill already supports the task and the remaining failures come from exploration or execution, graph editing need not help and can make short-budget behavior worse. The method’s value therefore depends as much on declining unsupported edits as on proposing useful ones.

H.3 NEXT STEPS 

The first priority is to extend the robustness audit with larger IID and distribution-shift splits, multi-optimization-seed factorial ablations, and alias-aware semantic scoring alongside strict scores. Ac-curacy should also be reported with provider-normalized token use, concurrency, and latency so that full-graph rendering and repeated Gates can be compared as systems. The second is finer attribution granularity. The current candidate–reference comparison already validates node fields and edges through object-level intervention; future systems can extend the same contract to exact text spans, theorem statements, table cells, or image crops, enabling equally targeted repair and rollback at finer addresses. Finally, failures should be routed to the component that can actually repair them. Repeated activation or role-selection errors belong in the skill graph; missing facts belong in retrieval; ambiguous digits belong in perception tools; persistent execution failures may require model updates; scorer-only mismatches require no model change. Evaluating this repair routing, including how often the system correctly chooses NO_PATCH , is more important than simply growing the graph. H.4 BROADER RESEARCH DIRECTIONS FOR SKILL -B ASED AGENTS 

The broader field is moving from demonstrating that skills can help toward understanding how a skill system should operate over time. Four research problems stand out. 

Scalable selection with semantic guarantees. Large libraries cannot be rendered in full, but similarity-only Top-K retrieval can omit prerequisites or retrieve a procedure outside its valid boundary. Graph-of-Skills, SkillDAG, HyperSkill, and CaSKG point toward dependency-aware and higher-order retrieval (Liu et al., 2026; Bai et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026c). The next step is to optimize selection, dependency completion, and context cost jointly, while measuring whether the retrieved subgraph contains every required step and excludes conflicting skills. 

Interventional credit assignment. Most skill updates are still justified by correlations between a trajectory and its outcome. Counterfactual removal, substitution, and reordering of skills or edges can test whether an object actually changed the result and can separate its individual effect from interactions with other skills (Li et al., 2026c; Jiang et al., 2026). Extending such interventions to long-horizon tasks would make delayed credit, failure attribution, and rollback robust to longer and more interacting execution paths. 

Lifecycle management rather than unchecked accumulation. As experience streams grow, the central problem is no longer only how to add a skill, but when to merge, split, specialize, retire, or restore one. SkillOS studies learned curation, SkillsBench shows that self-generated skills are not reliably beneficial, and boundary-aware memory emphasizes explicit applicability and avoidance 31 Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 conditions (Ouyang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a; Lin et al., 2026). A mature skill repository therefore needs versioned evidence, conflict tests, usage-aware pruning, and trust policies for imported skills rather than monotonic growth (Xu & Yan, 2026). 

Joint learning of models and external skills. External skills are easy to inspect and update, whereas model parameters offer compact execution and broad generalization. Work on trajectory distillation, reasoning memory, evolving memory skills, and co-evolution suggests treating them as two coupled learning channels rather than alternatives (Ni et al., 2026; Ouyang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026b;a). A useful research target is a closed loop that keeps uncertain or fast-changing pro-cedures external, internalizes repeatedly validated behavior, and then uses the improved model to generate and test the next generation of skills without amplifying its own errors. 32
